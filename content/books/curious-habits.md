Recently, I read a book about habits.  
The main idea of the book is not simply “how to build a habit” or “how to change yourself in 30 days”.

Instead, it tries to explain the mechanism behind habits.

Why is it so hard to change something, even when we clearly know it is good for us?  
Why do some behaviors become automatic, while others always feel painful and energy-consuming?

The answer given by the book is very interesting:

> Curiosity is the key.

## Habits Are Designed to Save Energy

One story mentioned in the book is about the sea squirt.

A sea squirt has something like a brain when it is still moving around.  
But once it finds a good place to stay and attaches itself there, it gradually digests its own brain. 


The book uses this as a metaphor for habits.

When a behavior becomes a habit, our brain no longer needs to spend too much energy thinking about it.  
This is actually useful. If we needed to think deeply about every tiny action every day, life would become extremely expensive from an energy perspective.

So in a way, habits are like an optimization mechanism.

They reduce mental cost.  
They make repeated behaviors easier.  
They allow us to run part of our life in “automatic mode”.

As an engineer, I think this is quite intuitive.  
A habit is almost like a cached execution path. Once the path is trained enough, the system can reuse it with lower runtime cost.

**But the dangerous part is also here. Not every cached path is still correct.**

Some habits may have been useful in the past, but they may no longer match our current goal.  
Some habits may have been built unconsciously. We may not even notice whether they are helping us or hurting us.

That is why the book keeps emphasizing curiosity.

We should occasionally stop and ask:

Why do I have this habit?  
What reward did it give me before?  
Is that reward still meaningful now?  
Does this behavior still match the person I want to become?

## Curiosity as a Debugging Tool

I like to think of curiosity as a debugging tool for life.

When we hit a bottleneck, the first reaction should not always be self-blame.

It is more useful to ask:

Why is this hard?  
What is triggering this behavior?  
What reward am I actually getting from it?  
Why do I keep repeating it, even though I know it may not be good for me?

This is similar to debugging a system.

If a program keeps producing the wrong output, simply shouting at the program is useless.  
We need to trace the input, check the state, find the trigger, and understand the hidden dependency.

Habits are similar.

Many behaviors are not random.  
**They usually follow some kind of loop: a cue, a behavior, and a reward.**

For example, the book also mentions ideas similar to those in *Atomic Habits*.  
If we want to read more, one simple method is to put a book on the bed before taking a shower. After the shower, the book becomes a visible cue. It gently pushes us toward reading.

Reward is another important part.

When an app gives us a streak, a score, or a small achievement badge, it is not just decoration.  
It creates a small reward signal. It gives the brain a feeling of progress, and that feeling helps the habit continue.

This also explains why building habits is so difficult.

**We are not only fighting laziness.  
We are trying to rewrite an existing behavior loop.**

## Changing a Habit Is Like Redirecting a River

One analogy from the book that I really like is the river analogy.

If a river has already been flowing in one direction for a long time, changing its path is not easy.  
At the beginning, it requires a lot of force.

The old path is already smooth.  
The new path is still rough.

But once the water starts flowing through the new path again and again, the new route becomes smoother.  
Eventually, it becomes natural.

I think this describes habit change very well.

At the beginning, a new habit always feels expensive.  
Exercise feels annoying.  
Reading feels slow.  
Sleeping earlier feels unnatural.  
Not checking the phone feels uncomfortable.

But this does not mean we are weak.

It only means the new path has not been trained yet.

Once we survive the early difficult stage, the behavior gradually becomes easier.  
The system starts to optimize itself.

## Do Not Build Someone Else’s Habit

**Another idea I like from the book is that we should not blindly copy other people’s goals.**

Many people think:

I want to have a body like that person.  
I want to be as disciplined as that person.  
I want to live exactly like that successful person.

But this kind of comparison can easily make habits unsustainable.

The book uses a beautiful idea: **every person is like a diamond covered in dirt.**

The goal is not to become someone else.  
The goal is to uncover our own value.

So when we build habits, we should not only ask, “What is the best habit?”  
We should ask, “What is the habit that fits me?”

For example, if I want to become healthier, it does not mean I must train exactly like a fitness influencer.  
Maybe swimming fits me better.  
Maybe walking fits me better.  
Maybe a short daily routine is more realistic than an aggressive training plan.

The important thing is not to copy the external shape of someone else’s life.

The important thing is to find a sustainable path that belongs to me.

## Habits Keep Evolving

One important reminder from the book is that habits are not fixed.

A habit may be useful at one stage of life, but become unnecessary later.  
A behavior may once give us a reward, but that reward may no longer exist.  
A routine may once protect us, but later become a limitation.

So we need to keep observing ourselves.

This is where curiosity becomes powerful again.

Instead of treating habits as permanent rules, we can treat them as living systems.  
They should evolve together with our goals, environment, and identity.

When I think about it this way, habit improvement becomes less like forcing myself to obey a rule.  
It becomes more like maintaining and refactoring a system.

Some parts are still useful.  
Some parts are legacy code.  
Some parts need to be redesigned.

And sometimes, the most important thing is simply to notice that the system has changed.

## A More Positive Way to Look at Ourselves

Near the end of the book, there is another reflection that I find meaningful.

Imagine the current version of yourself meeting the 15-year-old version of yourself.

Some people may immediately think:

You should have done this earlier.  
You should not have made that mistake.  
You should have worked harder.

But the book suggests a more positive way to think.

Maybe we should thank our younger self.

Because that person made many decisions, experienced many failures, and survived many difficult moments, we are here today.

In the same way, we can also ask:

What will my future self thank me for?

This question feels much better than pure self-criticism.

It does not mean every decision is perfectly right or wrong.  
Life is not that binary.

Every action gives us feedback.  
Every experience teaches us something.  
If we stay curious, we can keep learning from these experiences and adjust our path.

## Final Thoughts

For me, the most important message of this book is:

> A good habit is not necessarily the habit that looks impressive from the outside.  
> A good habit is the habit that truly fits your own life and helps you move toward your own goal.

Habits exist because the brain wants to save energy.  
This is not a bad thing. It is a natural optimization.

But we need curiosity to inspect these optimizations from time to time.

Some habits are still helping us.  
Some habits are outdated.  
Some habits were built unconsciously and need to be redesigned.

If we can stay curious, we can keep improving our habits without constantly comparing ourselves with others.

In the end, habit change is not about becoming another person.

It is about understanding ourselves more deeply, finding a path that fits us, and slowly becoming a better version of ourselves.
