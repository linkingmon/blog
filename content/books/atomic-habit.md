I think most people have already heard of *Atomic Habits* by James Clear. It is one of those books that has stayed popular for many years, so I was actually a little hesitant before reading it. I had seen some people say that the book was overrated, so I did not buy it immediately. However, after a friend recommended it to me, I decided to give it a try.

After reading it, **I think the biggest strength of this book is that its logic is very clear. The concepts are simple, the structure is easy to follow, and the examples are practical enough for most people to understand quickly.** I would not say it is the best book about habits, but I do think it is worth reading because it gives us a useful framework to examine our own behavior.

## Small Habits May Not Be Magic, But They Do Matter

At the beginning of the book, the author emphasizes the importance of small habits. He explains that tiny improvements can accumulate over time and eventually create a large effect.

To be honest, I am not completely convinced by the “compound effect” explanation. I think sometimes it may be more reasonable to describe habits as an additive process rather than a multiplier. A good habit does not always bring a dramatic change to our lives. Sometimes the result is just slow and ordinary.

But even so, I still believe habits are powerful.

The reason is not that every tiny action will magically become a huge success. The real value of habits is that they make good actions more natural. **When something becomes a habit, we spend less mental energy forcing ourselves to do it. We simply do it because it has become part of our routine.**

This is also why I like the idea of the “valley of disappointment.” When we work on something for a while but do not see obvious results, it is easy to feel that our effort is useless. But many things in life work this way. We may need to repeat the same action many times before the result becomes visible. The book uses a metaphor that I really like: a stonecutter may break the stone on the hundredth strike, but that does not mean the first ninety-nine strikes were meaningless.

## Changing Identity Instead of Only Chasing Results

One of the concepts I found useful is changing our identity.

For example, if two people are trying to quit smoking, they may say two different things:

“I am trying to quit smoking.”

“I do not smoke.”

These two sentences look similar, but the second one is more powerful because it implies a different identity. The person is not just fighting against a habit. They already see themselves as someone who does not smoke.

I think this is useful because many goals are too result-oriented. For example, “I want to read one book” is just a task. But “I want to become a person who reads” is an identity. The second one is more likely to influence our daily decisions.

After reading this part, I also tried to organize my own goals into labels. **Instead of only listing many goals for the year, I tried to ask myself what kind of person I wanted to become.** For example, I came up with labels like “language lover,” and “sports enthusiast.”

I also found a great example on a youtube called "瑀禾ON DUTY" where she utilized this method in [her 2024 planning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=irqGyf_7O-U#t=5m22s)

I think this process helped me clarify my direction. If we do not know what we want to do, I recommended first list many things we are interested in, then group them into common labels, and finally ask ourselves whether those labels really represent the person we want to become.

## The Four Steps of Behavior

The core framework of this book is the four steps of behavior:

**Cue, craving, response, and reward.**

For example, when my phone receives a notification: The cue is the notification sound. The craving is the desire to check it. The response is picking up the phone. The reward is the satisfaction of seeing the message.

I think this framework is useful because it gives us a way to analyze habits instead of only blaming ourselves for lacking discipline. **If we understand how a habit works, we can redesign each step.**

For good habits, we may (for bad habits the opssosite):
| steps | good (bad) habits |
|-|-|
|cue| **make cue obvious (invisible)**|
|craving| **make habit (un)attractive**|
|response|**make action easy (difficult)**|
|reward|**make reward (un)satisfying**|

This sounds simple, but I think the important point is not to memorize the four steps. The important point is to use them to inspect our own habits and customize methods that actually work for us.

## Making Cues Obvious

For good habits, the first step is to make the cue obvious.

One useful method is to become aware of our existing habits. We can list our daily behaviors and mark them as positive, negative, or neutral. For example, waking up may be neutral, checking the phone immediately may be negative, and brushing teeth may be positive. This helps because we often ignore small bad habits if we do not write them down.

**Another method is to use time and place to trigger a habit.** I think this is especially useful for me. Sometimes I want to do something, but because I do not have enough motivation, I keep delaying it. If I put it on my calendar and even tell a friend about it, I become more likely to do it.

A useful sentence structure is:

“I will do [action] at [time] in [place].”

For example, “I will go to the nearby gym every Tuesday evening.” This kind of sentence makes the habit more concrete, and it gives us less room to escape. The brain is very good at finding excuses; we need to make the contract clear before it starts lawyering up.

### Habit Stacking and Environment Design

Among the methods in the book, **habit stacking is one of the most effective for me.**

The basic idea is:

“After I do A, I will do B.”

The reason this works is that an existing habit becomes a cue for a new habit. After repeating the sequence many times, the connection becomes natural.

For example, if I want to read more, I can design a sequence like this:

Wake up → make the bed → put a book on the bed → take a shower → see the book → start reading.

The key is to find the right cue. It is not enough to simply say, “I want to read more.” We need to think about where this habit should be inserted into our daily routine.

Environment is also important. If I want to practice guitar more, I should put the guitar somewhere I can easily see it. If I put it inside a case in the corner, I should not be surprised that I never touch it.

I also feel this in my own life. Different spaces create different mental states. When I was a student, I tended to relax in my dorm, but when I went to the lab or a café, I became more focused. So **I think designing the environment is not just a small trick. It is a practical way to reduce the resistance of doing the right thing.**

## Making Habits Attractive, But Carefully

The book also introduces **temptation bundling**. The idea is to connect something we need to do with something we want to do.

For example: **After I finish the habit I need, I can do the habit I want**.

I once heard a joke that we usually have free dinner in speechs, and that is a type of temptation bundling.

This can be effective, but I think there is a risk. If the thing we want to do is actually a bad habit, we may end up overdoing it. For example, if I tell myself I can watch videos after studying, I may study for thirty minutes and then watch videos for three hours. That is not habit design. That is just opening the city gate and hoping the enemy behaves politely.

So I think temptation bundling only works when the benefit of the needed habit is greater than the cost of the wanted habit. Otherwise, the reward may become a new problem.

## Make Action Easy

### Action Is More Important Than Perfect Preparation

Another part I strongly agree with is the difference between being in motion and taking action.

**Sometimes we keep planning, researching, and preparing, but we never actually act. It feels productive, but it may just be a comfortable way to avoid failure or criticism.**

I believe action is the key. We can only gain real experience through action. Thinking without doing will not move us forward.

I once heard a sentence that I really like:

**A right decision is right.  
A wrong decision can also be right.  
Only making no decision is wrong.**

This is especially true in situations that require execution. In war, a commander cannot wait forever for a perfect decision. In companies, I think leaders also need the ability to act, adjust, and learn. Planning is useful, but compared with real execution, it is still limited.

### Frequency Matters More Than Duration

The book also mentions that frequency is more important than duration. For example, studying Korean for thirty minutes every day may be better than studying for three and a half hours once a week.

I think this makes sense, but I would explain it in a more practical way. If we spend too much time on a habit at once, we may become tired and build resistance toward it. This is especially true for habits that we want to build but do not naturally enjoy yet.

A better method is to find a duration that does not consume too much energy. If thirty minutes feels too long, we can start with twenty minutes, or even less. After doing it for a while, the resistance becomes lower, and then we can slowly increase the time.

This is what I did when I started learning Korean. At first, I really resisted it. Every time I thought about studying Korean, I felt it was difficult. But I started with the idea that action comes first. I used a timer and studied for thirty minutes a day. After three months, my monthly completion rate was around 80%. Looking back, I still find it surprising.

### I Prefer Using Time Instead of Quantity

When building a habit, I personally prefer using time instead of quantity.

For example, instead of saying “I must read ten pages every day,” I prefer saying “I will read for thirty minutes every day.”

The second method gives me less pressure. It makes me feel that I have already reserved this period for the habit. I do not need to rush just to meet a number. This also reduces anxiety and helps me focus more on the process.

## Making Reward Satisfying 

Because good habits usually have costs now and rewards later, while **bad habits often have rewards now and costs later, we need to make good habits more satisfying in the present.**

One method is **visualization**.

Some people may put money into a jar every time they complete a habit. Some may move a paper clip or marble from one box to another. I personally prefer using apps. I use a habit tracking app to record daily and weekly goals, and I like that it can show monthly completion rates.

Accountability is also useful, but I do not like making it too extreme. The book talks about habit contracts and punishments, but I think a more natural method is simply to share our goals with people we trust. When someone knows what we are trying to do, we may gain extra motivation to continue.

## From A to A+

In the final part of the book, the author talks about how to move from being good to becoming great.

One idea is:

**Habit + deliberate practice = mastery.**

I think this is reasonable. Habits help us repeat an action naturally, but if we want to improve further, we still need deliberate practice. Repetition alone is not enough. We need to review, adjust, and increase the difficulty when necessary.

This is why I think **regular review** is important. We should ask ourselves:

What went well?

What did not go well?

What did I learn?

What should I adjust next?

My current method is to set monthly goals. Near the end of each month, I check my habit tracking app, review the results, and think about how to improve my system for the next month.

## Final Thoughts

Overall, I think *Atomic Habits* is a useful book because it does not only give many random tips. Its real value is that it provides a framework: **cue, craving, response, and reward.**

After understanding this framework, we can design our own methods instead of copying every example from the book. This gives us more flexibility.

For me, the most important lessons are:

First, identity is more powerful than a single goal.

Second, habits should be designed, not forced.

Third, action is more important than perfect preparation.

Fourth, small habits may not change life overnight, but they can make progress more natural.

**In the end, I think building habits is not about becoming a perfect person. It is about making the right actions easier to repeat. If we can reduce resistance, review our progress, and keep adjusting our system, then even small actions can slowly move us toward the person we want to become.**
