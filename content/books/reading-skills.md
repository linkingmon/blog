First, let me explain why I wanted to read this book.

I have always liked collecting new knowledge. In my daily life, I spend a lot of time reading different kinds of media, including Facebook, LinkedIn, Instagram, Twitter, MIT Technology Review, and many other sources. Sometimes, when I do not know what to do, I just take out my phone and start scrolling. Then, without noticing it, a lot of time has passed.

So when I saw this book at a bookstore, I suddenly had a question: is there a more efficient way to read? Can I reduce the unnecessary time I spend browsing information, but still keep the same learning effect?

This book gives me a lot of useful ideas. I would not say every method in the book is suitable for everyone, but I think this kind of Japanese practical book is very useful because it provides many small techniques. When I read it, I usually find a few ideas that resonate with me, and then I try to integrate them into my original learning and time-management system.

## The Purpose of Reading Is to Gain Different Perspectives

One of the most important ideas in this book is that the purpose of reading is not just to collect information. **The real purpose is to gain different perspectives.**

I strongly agree with this. One event never has only one side. If we can increase the number of perspectives we have, we can understand the world with more depth and flexibility. Reading is not simply about knowing more facts. It is about building a richer structure in our mind.

The author divides media into different types. Some media are broad but shallow, while others are narrow but deep. Some are more neutral, while others are biased. **His suggestion is to first use broad and neutral media to understand the outline of an event, then use deeper sources to explore different viewpoints**, and finally look at more opinionated media to understand the full picture.

I think this is a very practical method. If we only read content that agrees with us, our thinking will easily become narrow. This is especially dangerous today, because social media algorithms tend to show us content similar to what we already believe. Over time, we may fall into an echo chamber without noticing it.

## How to Avoid Biased Information

The book also talks about how to identify biased media. I think this part is useful because we are surrounded by too much information every day, but not all information has the same quality.

Some warning signs are quite clear. For example, if an article explains an event in an overly simple way, always tries to create a villain, uses emotional language, or has unclear sources, then we should be careful. These articles may not be trying to help us understand reality. They may only be trying to trigger our emotions.

## Avoid Information bubbles
Ideas like **[alternative facts (另類事實)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_facts)**, **[post-truth (後真相)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-truth)**, and the **echo chamber effect** make this problem worse.

“Alternative facts” means treating false or misleading claims as another version of the truth.

“Post-truth” means emotions and personal beliefs can become more powerful than facts.

The “echo chamber effect” means people in the same group keep repeating similar views, making it harder to accept different opinions.

To avoid this, we should step outside our usual information circle and learn to read different views calmly and objectively.

## Collecting Information Actively

Because we receive too much passive information every day, the author suggests that we should actively build our own information sources.

For example, he recommends using RSS readers to collect articles from selected websites. I tried using Feedly before, and I think the concept is good. It removes unnecessary advertisements and helps us read information in a cleaner way. However, in my own case, I found that some platforms I often use, such as LinkedIn, are not easy to integrate into RSS readers. So I did not continue using it deeply.

Another method I prefer is subscribing to newsletters from sources I care about. For example, if I subscribe to MIT Technology Review, I can receive articles directly in my email. For most people, I think this is already enough. We do not need to build a perfect system from the beginning. A system that can actually be used is much more important than a perfect system that only exists in imagination.

The author also suggests dividing articles into different levels. Some articles only need us to read the title. Some can be skipped when we are busy. Some can be saved for later when we have time. I think this is a very good mindset. Not every article deserves the same amount of attention. Our attention is limited, so we should spend it carefully.

## Using Social Media to Find Reliable Knowledge

The book also explains how to use social media to collect knowledge. The author thinks social media is an important source of information, but we should not use it passively.

**One method he proposes is to observe how experts comment on an article.** We can check whether there are professionals discussing it, whether they use professional terms correctly, what their background is, and whether their language is calm and objective. If someone consistently gives reliable comments, we can add them to our trusted list.

I think the most important point here is that trust should not be permanent. **A person who was reliable in the past may not always be reliable in the future. We should keep observing people over time instead of blindly trusting a fixed authority.**

## How to Read Books

The author also talks about how to read books. He prefers ebooks because they are easier to store, search, copy, and carry. I agree with many of these points. Ebooks reduce the pressure of physical space, and the search function is very useful when we want to find something later.

However, I also think bookstores are still important. A good bookstore is not just a place that sells books. The way books are arranged can create unexpected connections. Sometimes, when we browse a shelf, we may find a book we did not know we needed. This kind of accidental discovery is hard to replace.

When choosing what to read, the author suggests that we should check whether the book fits us, understand our current reading ability, **try reading the first thirty pages, give up books that are not suitable, and first read books that make us happy.**

I like this idea. Reading should not always feel like a painful duty. Of course, **difficult books are important, especially classics, because they train us to see the world from different angles**. But if every reading experience becomes suffering, it is hard to maintain the habit for a long time.

**For practical books, I think the best method is to first scan the table of contents and choose the parts we actually want to read**. Practical books often contain many scattered ideas. We do not always need to read them from beginning to end. The goal is to absorb useful fragments and connect them with our own system.

## Remembering Through Feelings

One method I found especially interesting is to write down a short feeling when something attracts us.

For example, when reading, we can write notes like:

“This reminds me of another book.”

“This seems related to an article I read before.”

“Is this the same idea as XXX?”

I did not originally have this habit, but I think it is very useful. **Memory is not only semantic. It is also episodic. If we write down our feelings at the moment of reading, we may remember the content better because it is connected with a personal experience.**

This is also why I think reading notes should not only be summaries. **A summary records what the book says, but a feeling records how the book connects with us**. The second part is often more valuable.

## The Brain and the Computer Should Work Together

Another idea I strongly agree with is that the brain and the computer should store different things.

The computer is good at storing a large amount of detailed information. It can search by keywords or tags, and it does not forget. The human brain, on the other hand, is not good at storing every detail, but it is very good at forming concepts and creating new ideas.

So my own method is similar to the author’s idea. I prefer to keep concepts and structures in my brain, while storing detailed notes, examples, and references on my computer.

For example, if I read a very good example in a book, I do not need to memorize every sentence. I only need to remember that “this is a great example” and remember a few keywords. Later, when I need it, I can search for it on my computer.

**This method allows my brain to stay light. It does not need to carry too many details**. Instead, it can focus on connecting concepts. When enough concepts are stored in the brain, they may suddenly collide with each other and create new ideas. Sometimes, an idea appears like: “Oh, so A is actually related to F.”

I think this kind of unexpected connection is very important for creativity. It does not come from forcing ourselves to think harder. It comes from giving the brain enough materials and enough space.

Of course, this method may not suit everyone. **But for people who like thinking creatively, I think it is a very powerful approach.**

## Multitasking and Time Management

The book also discusses multitasking. I have read many books that say we should avoid multitasking, but I personally still use it. I think whether multitasking works depends on how well we can control our energy and resources.

If we understand our own mental state and can predict how much energy each task consumes, multitasking can help us control our mind better. It can even help generate creative ideas. But if we cannot control it, multitasking will only make our brain messy.

My original method was to build fixed habit groups. For example, I might reserve 7 to 8 a.m. for sketching, meditation, or writing notes. I do not assign only one fixed task because that would be too rigid. If I am not in the right mood, the plan may fail easily. But if I create a group of related habits, I have more flexibility.

The author’s idea of **combining heavy tasks with light tasks** makes this method more systematic. Heavy tasks are tasks that easily create fatigue, such as reading difficult books, writing serious articles, or organizing complex ideas. Light tasks are easier tasks, such as checking news titles, reading simple articles, watching videos, saving notes, or adjusting existing writing.

I think each person should define heavy and light tasks based on their own experience. For example, I once wanted to study cooking and sketching at night. After actually trying both, I found that I had more passion for cooking. So for me, cooking became a light task, while sketching became a heavy task.

This is also why I do not fully like the Pomodoro Technique. I have tried it before, but if a whole day is divided into too many thirty-minute blocks, managing time itself becomes another source of mental cost. My current method is to use Google Calendar to arrange one- or two-hour blocks for integrated habit groups, while still leaving some flexibility.

## Final Thoughts

Overall, I really like and recommend this book.

What I learned from it is not one single magical method, but a set of small techniques that can be integrated into my own system. The most important ideas for me are:

* Reading is about gaining perspectives.
* We need to avoid biased information and information bubbles.
* Good information should be collected actively.
* Books should be read with flexibility based on their type.
* The brain and the computer should work together.
* Time management should consider mental energy, not only clock time.
