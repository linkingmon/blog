I recently spent some time reading Morris Chang’s autobiography. Interestingly, I started from the second volume first, and then went back to the first volume later.

To me, **the second volume was more interesting and more rewarding**. The first volume was still special in its own way. It talked about his childhood, his family, the war, moving from place to place, changing schools many times, and eventually going to the United States. He first studied mechanical engineering at Harvard, and later transferred to MIT. His relatives also helped him during that period.

One thing I found quite interesting was that **Morris Chang actually liked literature. He was not just a pure “technology person” from the beginning. He read, wrote, and discussed literature with others**. In the first volume, what attracted me the most was not necessarily the main storyline of his childhood, but some of the small essays near the end. Those parts felt more personal and more alive to me.

But the second volume gave me much more to think about.

Before reading this book, I had a certain image of Morris Chang. I thought he must be someone who was extremely strong in politics, economics, and technology at the same time. After all, we often see him representing Taiwan in many international economic events. In the beginning of the  book, there are also many photos of him with Nobel Prize winners, political leaders, presidents, and important people from different countries.

So at first, I almost assumed that he must have been someone who deeply understood all these fields from the beginning. But after reading through his life story, I had a different feeling. He felt much more like a normal person than I expected.

Of course, I do not mean he was not talented. He was clearly very capable, disciplined, and hardworking. But his path was not as smooth or as “destined” as people might imagine.

For example, he originally wanted to get a PhD, but he failed the exam more than once. After failing several times, he decided to go to work. His background was mechanical engineering, so it would have been natural for him to work in a mechanical-related field. But because of different circumstances, he eventually entered the semiconductor industry.

Looking back today, it is very easy to say, “Wow, he had great vision. He chose semiconductors.” **But I am not sure that is the full truth. At that moment, it may not have been some grand strategic decision. It may have been a choice made under real-world limitations, opportunities, and coincidences. He entered a field, kept working in it, became good at it, and then the industry itself grew into something huge.**

This part made me think a lot. Sometimes when we look at successful people from the outside, we tend to imagine that every step was planned perfectly. But when we actually read their story, many things were not that clean. There were failures, accidents, timing, people who helped them, and environments that allowed them to grow.

His time at Texas Instruments was especially important to me. I feel that was probably the period where he grew the most. He was fortunate to meet good bosses and leaders who appreciated him. He received opportunities, trust, and positive feedback. Because of that, he could keep taking on bigger responsibilities and keep improving.

This does not reduce his achievement. Actually, I think it makes the story more realistic.

**A talented person still needs a good environment.** If you put someone in a place where they are trusted, challenged, and supported, they may grow far beyond what they originally imagined. But if the environment does not allow them to grow, even a very capable person may be stuck.

Later in his career at Texas Instruments, Morris Chang also experienced frustration. He did not reach the very top position. Instead, his role gradually became less important. He stayed for a while because he had loyalty to the company, but eventually he had to leave.

After that, **he tried other things, including venture capital and consulting, but those were not extremely successful. Even when he came to Taiwan and took leadership roles in institutions like ITRI, it was not immediately smooth.** There were cultural differences, organizational issues, and many things he had to adjust to.

So again, the story was not simply: a genius appears, sees the future, and builds TSMC. It was much messier than that. And maybe that is exactly why I found it meaningful.

TSMC’s foundry model was successful not only because Morris Chang was capable, but also because the timing, industry structure, Taiwan’s situation, and many other conditions came together. He had the experience from Texas Instruments. He had seen how the semiconductor industry worked from the inside. He had management experience, international vision, and the ability to organize resources. Then, when the right opportunity appeared in Taiwan, he was able to turn those accumulated experiences into something real.

So my biggest takeaway from this autobiography is not just “Morris Chang is great.” Of course he is great. **But my deeper feeling is: maybe great people are not as far from us as we imagine.**

**They are not born with a perfect map of the future. They also fail exams. They also make choices under uncertainty. They also need people to recognize them. They also need a good environment. They also experience frustration when the organization no longer values them.**

The difference may be that when they enter a field, they keep going. They keep learning. They keep accumulating judgment. And when the right opportunity finally appears, they are prepared enough to take it. This gives me a more grounded view of success.

Success is not only about talent. It is also about timing, environment, mentors, industry trends, and whether you happen to be in a place where your ability can be used.

But at the same time, luck only matters if you have prepared yourself enough to use it. Maybe the lesson is not to force ourselves to become a legendary person. Maybe the lesson is simpler.

**Find a field where we can keep working with curiosity and energy. Stay long enough to build real judgment. Try to put ourselves in environments where good feedback and good people exist. And when we meet someone who appreciates us, or when the timing becomes favorable, be ready to take the chance.**

Reading this book made Morris Chang feel less like a distant historical figure and more like a real person who kept walking through uncertainty.

That feeling is actually encouraging.

Because it means our own path may also be less fixed than we think. We may not know which choice will become important later. We may not know which job, which project, or which person will change our direction.

**As long as we keep doing meaningful work, keep learning, and keep putting ourselves near good opportunities, maybe one day some of those random-looking pieces will connect.**

And when that happens, people may look back and call it vision.

But while we are living it, it may simply feel like taking the next step.
